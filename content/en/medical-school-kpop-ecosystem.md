## What set this off

The thing in Korea right now is RESCENE.

They started as what Koreans call a "mom-and-pop idol" group. Small agency, tight budget, promotion they had to handle themselves. Starting from that, they're now the dominant group in Korean entertainment.

Let me state one thing up front. This post isn't written to trash RESCENE or the idol industry. The opposite, actually. So let me start with why I like them.

- **Woni** — adored for Karina-tier looks and still easygoing and honest. A leader whose charm is in the contrast.
- **Minami** — came all the way from Japan to be an idol in Korea, went through every kind of hardship, and never lost her thoughtfulness. She grows on you.
- **Liv** — tireless energy and enthusiasm that never grates. She puts the viewer in a good mood.
- **May** — cute chattering that fills the audio 24 hours a day, with thoughtful lines hidden inside it.
- **Zena** — just cute.

I had no particular interest in Korean idols and I've been deep in RESCENE videos lately. I assume most people arrived via roughly the same path.

Watching that long enough, at some point I got more curious about the industry structure behind the group than the group itself. What does an agency control and what does it give up when launching a group? Where's the bottleneck?

And then a completely unrelated legacy topic collided in my head. The med school rush.

---

## 1. The moment it gets named a "national disease," the discussion closes

There's a sentence that's hardened into near-consensus in Korea.

> "The best students all rush to medical school instead of engineering, and national competitiveness collapses."

The sentence itself is hard to rebut. Whether it's hard to rebut because the evidence is solid, or because rebutting it makes you a morally suspect person, is a separate question — and usually those two don't get distinguished.

Look again at the words already in use. **Rush** is a word for a failure of resource allocation. **Brain drain** means the departed resource is being used nowhere. So the frame already contains the conclusion. "This resource was wasted" is baked in as the premise.

I couldn't agree with that. Does concentrating a resource at one point produce only waste? Why doesn't the output produced at that concentrated point enter the calculation?

---

## 2. The most expensive variable from an agency's point of view

Observation first. When launching an idol group, the variables an agency can control split from the ones it can't.

- Songs, choreography, concept, producing → solvable with money and time
- Stage command, fandom response → probabilistic, but training reduces the variance
- Visuals → close to an inherited value, and high risk if you try to alter it after the fact

The third is the problem. The moment you try to alter it, medical side effects, recovery time, and variance in the outcome all enter as uncertainty. With a debut date locked in, that's a heavy computation.

To be clear up front: this is about the average risk structure of the industry as a whole, not about any particular group or person. What any individual did isn't information I have, and isn't information I need. What I'm looking at is what calculation an agency, as a decision-making entity, performs in the face of which variable.

From here it's interpretation. Korean agencies process this computation more cheaply than agencies elsewhere. The reason is simple: inside the narrow cluster of Gangnam there's already a high-density cosmetic-medicine ecosystem, built by top-tier personnel who converged there chasing expected returns and then competed with each other. Procedure pricing, recovery protocols, even the recovery path when something fails, are effectively standardized.

So the risk hasn't disappeared — there's **infrastructure that reduced the variance of the risk**. From a systems view that's a big difference. Same expected value, but high variance means you can't commit to a schedule.

Hypothesis: this infrastructure already functions as one of the back ends of K-pop as an export industry. It's the kind of asset other countries can't replicate quickly. (How much it actually contributed is hard to pin to a number — needs fact-checking.)

Counterfactual arguments of the "if they'd gone into engineering" variety aren't much use here. Nobody knows the GDP of the world line where they didn't. The only observable thing is what got built in the world line where they did.

---

## 3. Why doesn't this connection surface in mainstream discourse

This was more interesting. Looking only at the resource flow and the output, the causality isn't especially arcane, and yet I've almost never seen media or economics columns treat it as the main thread.

I think the reason is structural. Saying this connection out loud incurs two costs simultaneously.

1. K-pop gets locked into a "factory-line lookism" frame. You set out to praise the industry and end up trashing it.
2. You have to take the devil's advocate position against the hardened consensus that "med school rush = national disease."

So the proposition doesn't circulate not because of its truth value but because **the speaker's loss is guaranteed**. That's not individual intellectual laziness, it's the incentive structure of the discourse market.

---

## 4. Bringing in Kegan

While ping-ponging with an AI about this cognitive barrier, Robert Kegan's adult development theory came up.

Kegan divides adult consciousness development into stages. Most people, he argues, stay at stage 3 (having internalized the surrounding values) or stage 4 (having built one's own firm value system). Stage 4 is fairly mature, but it can't objectify the system itself.

One thing changes at stage 5 (the self-transforming mind). **You can treat even the system you believe in as one incomplete model.** Past the binary of good and evil, right and wrong, it becomes possible to simply observe the emergence produced by mutually contradictory systems tangling together.

Look at the med school rush only through the moral filter of "national loss" and something stays hidden; turn the filter off and it looks like this.

> High-performance, non-standard infrastructure produced by a local optimization of resources.

Worth flagging: this view doesn't mean "so the med school rush is good." That would be reattaching the opposite moral judgment — not turning off the filter but swapping it. What I'm getting at is closer to: there's a trajectory visible only while judgment is suspended.

---

## 5. A hypothesis that the architect's lens may be a training tool

Getting this far, a hypothesis formed.

**Doing computer science and designing system architecture may itself be decent training for approaching this stage-5 kind of vision.**

Designing a system leaves no room to empathize with a particular module or assign it morality. The work is this:

- calculate the input and output of resources
- avoid deadlock between nodes
- stare daily at side effects produced by independently written logic tangling together
- start by acknowledging emergent behavior nobody intended as *behavior* first, not as a bug

The last item feels like the core. When a system produces unexpected behavior, the first job is reproducing "why it came out." Saying "this shouldn't happen" solves nothing. Accumulate that habit for a few years and, looking at social phenomena, the sentence "this phenomenon shouldn't exist" naturally slides to the back.

There's an opposite risk, of course. Reduce everything to structure and you stop seeing the people actually getting hurt inside that system. The architect's lens is strong at observation and weak at allocating responsibility. The point that capability and responsibility have to be looked at separately is something I probably need to apply to myself here.

- When is looking with the moral filter off useful, and when is it dangerous? I haven't drawn that line yet.
