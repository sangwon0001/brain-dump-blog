## Where this starts — it's a meta problem, not a governance problem

The previous post established that a merge isn't a code change, it's
**the power to change the system's rules**.

One important distinction falls out of that.

> Governance decides outcomes.
> **Meta-governance decides who gets the standing to decide.**

The real problem in this system isn't the merge, it's
**where merge power arises and how it shifts**.

---

## Governance isn't voting, it's the flow of power

The voting structure is just the surface.

The core is:

- how power is generated
- how power accumulates
- how fast power moves
- whether power destabilizes the system

Meta-governance is ultimately
**a structure for controlling the flow of power**.

---

## Conditions meta-governance has to satisfy

There are conditions this structure has to meet.

- minimal human intervention
- suppress concentration of power
- maintain long-term stability
- keep incentives balanced
- suppress populism
- let the system correct itself

That's less democracy design than
**self-stabilizing system design**.

---

## The balance problem — ability and power aren't the same thing

In societies as in systems, big advances often come from a small number of exceptional actors.
But the moment that influence locks in and stops moving, the structure stiffens and the balance can break.

The point isn't to suppress ability, it's
**a structure that keeps the power ability produces from setting in place**.

Meta-governance's job isn't to remove power, it's
**to keep power flowing without hardening on one side**.

---

## Power generation — a single axis fails

If power comes from exactly one criterion, you get capture.

Possible signals:

- validation / security contribution
- track record of accurate judgment
- system contribution
- responsibility borne
- activity volume

Power ends up not as a single criterion but as
**a dynamically weighted structure over multiple signals**.

---

## Rate of power movement — the most important variable

Power can't be fixed in place, but if it shifts too fast the system becomes unstable.

Properties you need:

- Rate-limited change
- Inertia
- Gradual shift
- Feedback-driven adjustment

Meta-governance isn't a static structure, it's
**a dynamic structure that moves slowly**.

---

## Economic structure — the system's survival condition

For the system to hold:

> Attack ROI < Defense ROI

Participating in defense has to be rational, and attacking has to be irrational.

---

## A game-theory view — stability doesn't arrive on its own

This isn't a technical problem so much as a problem of strategic interaction between participants.

An attacker stands to gain a lot from capturing the system, while an individual defender may lose relatively little.
In that kind of **payoff asymmetry**, participation in defense drops and the system gets progressively more fragile.

Meta-governance isn't just distributing power — it has to include
**a game structure where defense is the long-run rational choice over attack**.

---

## Anti-populism — a short-term majority is dangerous

What a short-term majority picks can collide with long-term stability.

- immediate effect ❌
- delay ⭕
- damping ⭕
- multiple signals ⭕

Governance isn't a popularity contest, it's
**a mechanism for maintaining long-term stability**.

---

## Real-world constraint — protocols meet resistance

When economic power gets redistributed, resistance naturally follows.

The core lesson of Ethereum's EIP-1559:

- protocol change is a question of interests more than logic
- resistance is rational
- the system doesn't eliminate resistance
- **it absorbs resistance while moving**

Meta-governance can't be an idealized structure — it has to be
**a structure that includes resistance**.

---

## Self-stabilizing governance

There's no perfect stability.

But a system can:

- suppress concentration of power
- recover from errors
- keep incentives balanced
- avoid collapse

The goal is **dynamic stability, not static stability**.
