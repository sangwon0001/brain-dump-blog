## What set this off

Scrolling the news on the MRT into work again today, two stories snagged me. (Filed under "banter" — really it's me rambling to myself.)

One: the family in the Gangneung unintended-acceleration case is demanding the manufacturer hand over the "ECU specification." The other: a request that schools let Muslim students eat halal meals even if the families pay extra — and the comment section under it.

They look like completely unrelated stories. To me they were one thing. **A structural flaw in the closed system we're currently running.** One is about a system that can't eject a lethal bug. The other is about a system that can't absorb an exception.

Fair warning: today's post is harsher than usual. Section 1 in particular is not an open-ended conclusion.

---

## 1. Belief in unintended acceleration is not a bug you can coexist with

The Gangneung case involved a grandmother driving with her grandson in the car. The tragedy itself is awful. That part stands on its own.

What the family demanded this time is disclosure of the "ECU specification." It's a card thrown knowing full well the company can't produce it — it's a trade secret. Demand the undeliverable, and when it isn't delivered, "they're hiding something" follows automatically. That isn't an argument, it's **the structure of a public-opinion play**. (Details of the case need fact-checking.)

But what actually snagged me isn't the lawsuit. It's what comes after.

A person who believes in unintended acceleration, in the moment they actually hit the wrong pedal, **removes their own mistake from the candidate list first**. Then, on top of the certainty that "I am pressing the brake," they keep flooring the accelerator. That is how a long list of pedal-misapplication crashes happened.

This isn't just a false belief. It's a structure where **the belief wires straight into the control input**. I think it's the result of the unintended-acceleration YouTubers hard-coding a broken algorithm into people's heads through fear marketing.

Which is why I can't put this on the same shelf as ordinary conspiracy theories.

- Flat Earthers are exasperating. But the belief doesn't kill anyone.
- Same for blind religiosity. It's a harmless bug the system **can coexist with**.
- Belief in unintended acceleration is different. It **produces physical errors** on a shared system called the road.

That's where "potential perpetrator" comes from. Someone who might hit my family because of what they believe. I think a belief like this has to be cleared out of the system — through education, licensing, whatever the mechanism ends up being.

Instead, society wraps these people in an underdog frame and extends unlimited generosity. Because they're the weak party, because they're a bereaved family, because it's sad.

A system that carries an error threatening other people's lives because the carrier is "the weak party." I keep wondering whether that's a properly designed system.

## 2. Why a halal lunch request ends in hate

From there we moved to the second story.

The request is simple. **"We'll pay extra — let us eat halal food at school."**

The comments were, predictably, wall-to-wall abuse of Muslims. What's interesting is that the pattern was identical in the communities that normally put diversity and equality front and center.

> "Aren't they just making a fuss over religion?"

Explaining it as baseline anti-Muslim sentiment gets you half the answer. But if that were all of it, the identical reaction from the other side of the aisle wouldn't be explained. I think the other half sits in **the free school lunch system itself**.

So I pulled the religion variable out entirely and stress-tested only the logic.

Say there's a student with a **meat allergy**.

- For twelve years this kid eats meals with the meat dish removed. One or two vegetable sides.
- The parents paid exactly the same taxes as everyone else. But only their kid eats a thin meal. Is that fair?
- Pack a lunch every day for twelve years, then? Is it right that the cost and effort land entirely on the individual?

One or two kids per school, and you can wave it through as exception handling. So what happens when that population is 5% of the total? Does "you people should just absorb unequal welfare" still hold?

Here's the rebuttal people reach for most often.

> "Then the lunch ladies are the ones who get worked harder."

I think that sentence is the core of this post. **That rebuttal only appears when you insist on solving the problem inside the closed system.** Fixed facilities, fixed staff, fixed budget — draw the picture that way and it becomes a pie split, where anyone getting more means someone getting less.

That's the essence of free school lunch. It **structurally generates a situation where everyone else eats for free and you don't eat properly**. The people who lose out are always a minority, and the majority intensely dislikes any inconvenience to what they already enjoy.

That friction is the real fuel for the hate. Hate isn't the cause — **the system is continuously manufacturing it**.

## 3. This needs an architecture rewrite, not a patch

The fix is actually simple. **Throw the closed system out.**

The cleanest option I see is abolishing free school lunch or replacing it wholesale with a voucher system. Instead of handing out a fixed tray, let individuals pick what fits their conditions.

I'm setting aside the ideological question of selective versus universal welfare. Independent of that: if we actually want to move toward embracing diversity, **piling on more exception handling won't get us there.** Every added exception makes the original system stiffer, and the cost comes out as internal conflict.

I know it's a blunt, destructive fix. But I think we're past the point where patches hold.

## 4. The same pattern keeps repeating

Once you have this shape in hand, older things start looking identical.

### Pandemic relief payments

Trillions of won released to hand every citizen 400,000. Do the math and the money released comes back as inflation and taxes. **The state deliberately constructed a situation where not taking it is a guaranteed loss.**

And then people who had argued "don't hand out relief payments" got mocked with "said don't give it out, but didn't say they wouldn't take it." The loss structure was imposed by the system, in plain sight, and behaving rationally inside it got called hypocrisy.

That's what happens when you can't separate the layer of an argument from the layer of an action.

### Free subway rides for seniors

There's a senior living in Seoul who gets the full benefit of the infrastructure, and a senior living in a rural area or on the outskirts for whom just reaching a subway station is a project.

If the latter demands "let us ride buses free too," is that selfish?

Or was **the scheme itself — one that routes the benefit only to people with access to one specific piece of infrastructure** — badly designed from the start?

I go with the latter. And the fix has the same shape: instead of designating a vehicle and making it free, attach the benefit to the person.
